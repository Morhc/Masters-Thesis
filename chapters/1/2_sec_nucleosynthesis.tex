\section{Stars and the Origin of the Elements}

Stars are the primary location where the elements are formed by nuclear fusion, but they do not form all of the elements at the same time in their life cycle.
All stars begin their lives by fusing hydrogen to helium in their cores as part of the main sequence.
However, how much they produce is dependent on their initial mass. 
Low mass stars, those lighter than 3 solar masses, will primarily produce helium for their entire lives. 
After they exhaust the hydrogen in their cores, they will puff up into red giants and no longer fuse hydrogen in their cores, but begin burning hydrogen in a shell structure above the center of the star.
Eventually, they will become degenerate in their cores and undergo what is known as a helium flash and suddenly begin to fuse helium into carbon and oxygen in their cores as they continue to evolve as asymptotic giant branch (AGB) stars.
As AGBs, they will stop fusing helium in their cores, but continue to burn helium in a shell surrounding the core.
When this happens, the convective envelope of the star can expand and mix some hydrogen into the helium burning shell and the star will begin to flash again in what is known as a thermal pulse.
During these thermal pulses, a large number of neutrons are released and the star can produce a large number of the elements heavier than iron.
Eventually, the star will shed its outer layers and leave behind either a planetary nebula or white dwarf and they cease to produce new elements.
Intermediate mass stars, those between 3 and 8 solar masses, will not undergo a helium flash, but will instead begin to fuse helium into carbon and oxygen in their cores after they exhaust the hydrogen in their cores.
After they exhaust the helium in their cores, they can also fuse carbon into neon, but do not form elements beyond this, and will become white dwarfs.
\citep{prialnikIntroductionTheoryStellar2009, herwigEvolutionSolarIntermediateMass2013}.

Mass stars, heavier than 8 solar mass, also begin their lives by fusing hydrogen to helium in their cores, but they continue to fuse into heavier and heavier elements as they evolve. 
After they exhaust hydrogen in their cores, they will begin to fuse helium into carbon and oxygen in their cores.
Then they will continue to fuse carbon, neon, oxygen, and eventually silicon.
Once they begin to fuse silicon in their cores, they will begin to produce iron. 
Once iron begins to form in the core of stars, they begin to collapse as the fusion of elements heavier than iron is endothermic and does not produce energy.
This collapse cause the star to explode in a supernova and many nuclear reactions can occur.
After the supernova, the remnant core of the star can either become a neutron star or a black hole depending on the mass before collapse \citep{woosleyEvolutionExplosionMassive2002,prialnikIntroductionTheoryStellar2009}.

The rest of the elements heavier than iron that are not produced in AGB stars are largely produced during the merger of two neutron stars.
When two neutron stars merge, they produce extremely high densities of neutrons and the heaviest stable elements in the universe are formed \citep{kobayashiOriginElementsCarbon2020}.


\subsection{Massive Star Evolution}
Stars with an inital mass greater than $8~\Msun$ undergo a series of nuclear burning phases:
\[
\mathrm{H} \rightarrow \mathrm{He} \rightarrow \mathrm{C} \rightarrow \mathrm{Ne} \rightarrow \mathrm{O} \rightarrow \mathrm{Si} \rightarrow \mathrm{Fe} \rightarrow \text{core collapse}.
\]
These burning phases lead to the structure of the star being separated convective shells with primary burning structures, as seen in Figures \ref{fig:Prialnik_Fig9.19} and \ref{fig:fullkipp}.

After the formation of the Fe-core the star begins to rapidly contract as nuclear burning no longer can balance the gravitational force.
This leads to a core collapse supernova explosion where further shockwave induced nucleosynthesis can occur \citep{woosleyEvolutionExplosionMassive2002}.
The core of the star leaves behind a neutron star or black hole, and all other material beyond the mass cut is ejected into the interstellar medium.
The exact border of where mass is ejected from changes the yields of massive star nucleosynthesis.
\cite{ritterNuGridStellarData2018} place this mass cut for massive stars around the interface of the Si/O-shell.

\subsection{O-C Shell Mergers}\label{sec:oc_mergers_intro}

The burning shells of stars, as shown in Figures \ref{fig:Prialnik_Fig9.19} and \ref{fig:fullkipp}, are separated by radiative regions.
Because of this, there is no material shared between the shells, but in the final stages of advanced O-shell stellar burning, hours before the core collapse of the star, anywhere from 20\% to 40\% of 1D massive star models have been found to have a merger of the O-, Ne-, and C-shells \citep{rauscherNucleosynthesisMassiveStars2002, ritterConvectivereactiveNucleosynthesisSc2018, collinsPropertiesConvectiveOxygen2018, robertiGprocessNucleosynthesisCorecollapse2023, robertiOccurrenceImpactCarbonOxygen2025}.
This phenomenon is known as an "O-C shell merger".
Figure \ref{fig:kippenhahn_ritter} shows a Kippenhahn diagram of the merger of the O- and C-burning shells for a $15~\Msun$ $Z=0.02$ model from \cite{ritterNuGridStellarData2018}.
These mergers occur when the convective O- and C-shells grow large enough to overlap, forming a single extended convective region.
This happens when the upper boundary of the O-shell starts to grow and ingest some of the C-shell material.
As this material enters the O-shell, it begins to burn at the higher temperatures of the O-shell and the compositions of the shells begin to mix.

These mergers are known to have peculiar markers of nucleosynthesis because the convective-reactive environment of the O-shell, such as the production of the odd-Z elements P, Cl, K, and Sc which are underproduced in galactic chemical evolution calculations \citep{ritterConvectivereactiveNucleosynthesisSc2018,robertiOccurrenceImpactCarbonOxygen2025}.
The O-C shell merger has also been found to produce the \pnucn{}, heavy \nt-deficient isotopes, by photo-disintegrating stable heavy isotopes \citep{rauscherNucleosynthesisMassiveStars2002, ritterConvectivereactiveNucleosynthesisSc2018, robertiGprocessNucleosynthesisCorecollapse2023}.

O-C shell mergers have also been understood to be important for seeding asymmetries important for the supernova.
The shell mergers are highly asymmetric and provide perturbations necessary for the supernova explosion in the O-shell \citep{mullerStatusMultiDimensionalCoreCollapse2016, collinsPropertiesConvectiveOxygen2018, mullerHydrodynamicsCorecollapseSupernovae2020, yadavLargescaleMixingViolent2020a, andrassy3DHydrodynamicSimulations2020}.

\subsection{Hydrostatic Burning in Massive Stars}

Burning in stars largely occurs in hydrostatic equilibrium, where the energy produced by nuclear reactions balances the gravitational forces acting on the star.
This balance allows the star to maintain a stable structure and prevents it from collapsing under its own gravity.
The burning phases in stars occur at specific range of temperatures and densities, depending on the initial mass of the star as shown in Table \ref{tab:massivestarsburning}.
Much of the nuclear burning in the core and shells of stars happens in convective regions which is a region where the stratification of the star is unstable and the material mixes.  
In this section, the details of the hydrostatic burning phases in massive stars are described in more detail, along with the nuclear reactions that occur during these phases.

The following section describes the burning stages following \citet{woosleyEvolutionExplosionMassive2002} and \citet{iliadisNuclearPhysicsStars2015}.
H-burning is the first stage of hydrostatic burning which occurs in stars, primarily by the CNO cycle.
\[
    ^{12}\mathrm{C}(p,\gamma)^{13}\mathrm{N}(\beta^+)^{13}\mathrm{C}(p,\gamma)^{14}\mathrm{N}(p,\gamma)^{15}\mathrm{O}(\beta^+)^{15}\mathrm{N}(p,\alpha)^{12}\mathrm{C}
\]
In the CNO cycle, carbon, nitrogen, and oxygen act as a catalyst for the fusion of four $^{1}\mathrm{H}$ isotopes into $^{4}\mathrm{He}$. 
If the star does not have enough carbon, nitrogen, and oxygen to catalyze the fusion of hydrogen, it will instead fuse hydrogen into helium by the p-p chain.
\[
    \pt(\pt,e^+\nu)\hydrogen[2](\pt,\gamma)\helium[3](\helium[3],2\pt)\helium[4]
\]
After the hydrogen in the core of the star is used up, the star will begin to burn helium by two main processes that creates carbon and oxygen:
\[
    3\times\helium[4] \rightarrow ^{12}\mathrm{C} \quad \mathrm{and~then} \quad ^{12}\mathrm{C}(\alpha,\gamma)^{16}\mathrm{O}
\]
After helium is exhausted, the massive star will begin to fuse carbon either in its core or slighly above the core and primarily produce neon, magnesium, and sodium:
\[
    ^{12}\mathrm{C} + ^{12}\mathrm{C} \rightarrow ^{20}\mathrm{Ne} + \alpha
\]
\[
    ^{12}\mathrm{C} + ^{12}\mathrm{C} \rightarrow ^{23}\mathrm{Na} + p
\]
\[
    ^{12}\mathrm{C} + ^{16}\mathrm{O} \rightarrow ^{24}\mathrm{Mg} + \alpha
\]
\[
    ^{12}\mathrm{C} + ^{12}\mathrm{C} \rightarrow ^{24}\mathrm{Mg} + n 
\]
When the carbon burning stops, the star will begin to burn neon in the core which produces oxygen and magnesium by photo-disintegration:
\[
    ^{20}\mathrm{Ne}(\gamma,\alpha)^{16}\mathrm{O}
\]
\[
    ^{20}\mathrm{Ne}(\alpha,\gamma)^{24}\mathrm{Mg}
\]
After neon burning is complete, the star will begin to fuse oxygen in its core into silicon, sulfur, phosphorus, and magnesium:
\[
    ^{16}\mathrm{O}+^{16}\mathrm{O}\rightarrow^{28}\mathrm{Si} + \alpha
\]
\[
    ^{16}\mathrm{O} + ^{16}\mathrm{O} \rightarrow ^{31}\mathrm{S} + n
\]
\[
    ^{16}\mathrm{O} + ^{16}\mathrm{O} \rightarrow ^{31}\mathrm{P} + p
\]
\[
    ^{16}\mathrm{O} + ^{16}\mathrm{O} \rightarrow ^{30}\mathrm{Si} + 2p
\]
\[
    ^{16}\mathrm{O} + ^{16}\mathrm{O} \rightarrow ^{24}\mathrm{Mg} + 2\alpha
\]
With a core composed of \silicon[28] and \sulfur[32], these isotopes will undergo photo-disintegration reactions $(\gamma,\alpha)$, $(\gamma,\nt)$, and $(\gamma,p)$.
Once enough \helium[4] is produced by these photo-disintegration reactions, there is a net production of the elements up to iron and nickel:
\[
    ^{28}\mathrm{Si}(\alpha,\gamma)^{32}\mathrm{S}(\alpha,\gamma)^{36}\mathrm{Ar}(\alpha,\gamma)^{40}\mathrm{Ca}(\alpha,\gamma)^{44}\mathrm{Ti}(\alpha,\gamma)^{48}\mathrm{Cr}(\alpha,\gamma)^{52}\mathrm{Fe}(\alpha,\gamma)^{56}\mathrm{Ni}
\]

\subsection{Neutron Captures}

Although neutron captures are not a large part of the nucleosynthesis in massive stars, they are an important part of how the elements heavier than iron are formed.
In the neutron capture process, an iron nucleus in a neutron rich environment captures neutrons by $(\nt,\gamma)$ reactions and becomes increasingly \nt-rich and unstable.
The unstable nucleus then $\beta^-$ decays until it becomes a stable isotope.
The neutron capture processes are divided into three main regimes: the slow, intermediate, and rapid neutron capture processes, commonly referred to as the $s$, $i$, and $r$ processes.
The $s$ and $r$ processes were originally proposed by \cite{burbidgeSynthesisElementsStars1957} and the $i$ process was later identified by \cite{cowanProduction14CNeutrons1977}.
The name of each process refers to the speed at which the neutron captures occur compared to the $\beta^-$ decay timescale.
The faster the neutron captures occur, the more unstable the nucleus becomes and it travels further from the valley of stability.
Finally, a neutron shell becomes full when a nucleus has $N=50$, $82$, or $126$ neutrons, which corresponds to Sr/Y/Zr, Ba/La/Ce/Nd, and Pb/Bi.
When this happens, the $(\nt,\gamma)$ cross section becomes lower, making it more likely that the nucleus will undergo $\beta^-$ decay rather than capture another neutron.

The \sprn{} primarily occurs in AGB stars during their thermal pulse phase. Periodic He-shell flashes generate a convective zone in the He-intershell, which mixes freshly synthesized \carbon[12]. After the pulse, the convective envelope can penetrate into this region (third dredge-up), bringing carbon and other products to the surface.
Between pulses, a \carbon[13] pocket is formed when protons partially mix into the \carbon[12]-rich intershell, leading to \carbon[13] production via $^{12}\mathrm{C}(p,\gamma)^{13}\mathrm{N}(\beta^+)^{13}\mathrm{C}$.
The \carbon[13] subsequently reacts via $^{13}\mathrm{C}(\alpha,n)^{16}\mathrm{O}$, releasing neutrons with densities of $10^6{-}10^{10}~\mathrm{cm}^{-3}$, driving the \spr{} nucleosynthesis close to the valley of stability. 
Additional neutrons are briefly produced during He-shell flashes from \neon[22] which comes from $^{14}\mathrm{N}(\alpha,\gamma)^{18}\mathrm{F}(\beta^+)^{18}\mathrm{O}(\gamma,\alpha)^{22}\mathrm{Ne}$ and releases neutrons by $^{22}\mathrm{Ne}(\alpha,n)^{25}\mathrm{Mg}$ \citep{snedenNeutroncaptureElementsEarly2008,herwigEvolutionSolarIntermediateMass2013}.
In massive stars, a weaker form of the \sprn{} occurs during the He- and C-burning phases where neutrons are released by $^{22}\mathrm{Ne}(\alpha,\nt)^{25}\mathrm{Mg}$ \citep{pignatariWEAKSPROCESSMASSIVE2010,kappelerProcessNuclearPhysics2011}.

The \iprn{} has two sites considered for its production: metal poor AGB stars and rapidly accreting white dwarfs.
If an AGB star is metal poor, when it undergoes its third thermal pulse it can sustain a longer connection between the H and He rich layers and produce neutron densities of $10^{13}{-}10^{15}\cm^{-3}$ \citep{choplinIntermediateNeutronCapture2021}.
If a white dwarf has a main sequence star binary companion, it can accrete the H-rich material from its companion.
As hydrogen is accreted onto the white dwarf, it burns and creates a \helium[]-shell.
When the \helium[]-shell reaches a critical mass, it will undergo a flash and release neutrons by $^{13}\mathrm{C}(\alpha,n)^{16}\mathrm{O}$ \citep{denissenkovImpactNgReaction2021}.
\ipr{} nucleosynthesis has a similar peak abundance pattern to the \sprn{} as it does not travel significantly farther from the valley of stability, the primary difference being which isotope is the most abundant \citep{choplinIntermediateNeutronCapture2021}.

The \rprn{} is thought to occur in the merger of two neutron stars, as when they merge they produce a large number of neutrons at densities greater than $10^{20}\cm^{-3}$.
The neutron captures in the \rprn{} occur so rapidly that the nuclei produced are extremely unstable and travel far from the valley of stability.
The \rprn{} abundance distribution shows peaks at $A=80$, $A=130$, and $A=195$. 
The $A=130$ and $A=195$ peaks come from the fission of \nt-heavy actinides \citep{arnouldRprocessStellarNucleosynthesis2007, gorielyImpactWeakInteractions2015,mumpowerVdelayedFissionRprocess2018}.
A weaker form of the \rprn{} may occur in the supernova of a massive star which produces isotopes up to $A=130$ \citep{woosleyAlphaProcessRProcess1992, kobayashiOriginElementsCarbon2020}.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{$p$}{p}-Nuclei Nucleosynthesis}\label{sec:pnuclei_intro}

The \pnucn{} are a set of 35 \nt-deficient stable isotopes from \selenium[74] to $^{196}\mathrm{Hg}$ that make up a small fraction of the observed solar abundance distribution of their respective elements \citep{rappSensitivityPProcessNucleosynthesis2006,rauscherConstrainingAstrophysicalOrigin2013}.
These isotopes are typically 10{-}1000 times less abundant than their stable \nt-rich counterparts as seen in Figure \ref{fig:Rapp_2006}.
\cite{burbidgeSynthesisElementsStars1957} first identified these isotopes as a distinct group, and did not think that the \pnucn{} were produced by the neutron capture processes.
Instead, they proposed that a series of ($p,\gamma$) reactions in equilibrium with ($\gamma,p$) and ($\gamma,n$) reactions in the H-rich envelope of a Type II supernova in a process they labelled the "$p$ process".
This is because the \pnucn{} are shielded from neutron captures by stable isotopes, as shown in Figure \ref{fig:Rauscher2013_Fig1}.

\begin{figure}
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{chapters/1/figures/Rauscher2013_Fig1.png}
    \caption{Taken from \cite{rauscherConstrainingAstrophysicalOrigin2013}: The $p$ isotopes are shielded from \rprn{} decay chains by stable isotopes and are bypassed in the \sprn{} reaction flow.
    \label{fig:Rauscher2013_Fig1}}
\end{figure}

Since the proposal of the $p$ process by \cite{burbidgeSynthesisElementsStars1957}, the common understanding is that the observed solar abundance of the 35 \pnucn{} is produced by a superposition of astrophysical processes.
Some of the varied processes and sites that can produce the \pnucn{} include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \selenium[74]${-}$\molybdenum[94] can be produced in the ejecta of a supernova during the $\alpha$ capture process by photo-disintegrations \citep{woosleyAlphaProcessRProcess1992}.
    \item \selenium[74]${-}$\cadmium[108] can be produced by $\nu$ induced $(\nt,\pt)$ reactions in the winds of a supernova \citep{frohlichNeutrinoInducedNucleosynthesisA>642006,arconesProductionLightelementPrimary2011}.
    \item \strontium[84]${-}$\ruthenium[98] can be produced in a H-rich accretion disk around a neutron star by rapid ($p,\gamma$) and $\beta^+$ decay analogous to the neutron capture process \citep{schatzEndPointRp2001}.
    \item \gadolinium[152], \erbium[164], and \tantalum[180m] have significant contributions from the \sprn{} \citep{bisterzoSprocessLowmetallicityStars2011}.
    \item $^{113}\mathrm{In}$ and $^{115}\mathrm{Sn}$ can be made during the $r$ process \citep{dillmannPProcessSimulationsModified2008}.
    \item $\nu$ capture reactions have been found to play a role in the production of $^{113}\mathrm{In}$, $^{138}\mathrm{La}$, and $^{180\mathrm{m}}\mathrm{Ta}$ \citep{gorielyPuzzleSynthesisRare2001, arnouldPprocessStellarNucleosynthesis2003,sieverdingNProcessLightImproved2018}.
\end{itemize}

The majority of the \pnucn{} can be produced by the "\gprn{}", a process that was first proposed by \cite{woosleyPprocessesSupernovae1978} who did not think the temperatures of the H-envelope during a supernova were sufficient.
The \gprn{} is a secondary process that requires pre-existing stable nuclei that undergo photo-disintegrations $(\gamma,\nt)$, $(\gamma,\alpha)$, and $(\gamma,\pt)$ reactions to produce the \pnucn{} at temperatures of $1.5{-}3 \times 10^9 \mathrm{K}$ \citep{rauscherConstrainingAstrophysicalOrigin2013}. 
Because it is a secondary process, the \gprn{} is dependent on the metallicity of the star and seeds available \citep{travaglioTestingRoleSNe2015, battinoHeavyElementsNucleosynthesis2020}.

One site where the \gprn{} could take place is in the explosive O and Ne burning shells of a massive star \citep{woosleyPprocessesSupernovae1978,rayetPprocessRevisited1990,rauscherNucleosynthesisMassiveStars2002}.
In this site, the \pnucn{} are produced, but there are serious underproductions of the light isotopes, especially \molybdenum[92,94] and \ruthenium[96,96], \lanthanum[138], and \tantalum[180m] \citep{rappSensitivityPProcessNucleosynthesis2006,rauscherConstrainingAstrophysicalOrigin2013,pignatariProductionProtonrichIsotopes2016}.

Another site is the explosion of white dwarfs during Type Ia supernova if the seeds for the process are enhanced.
White dwarfs have \spr{} material from either during the prior AGB phase or from He-flashes during accretion from a companion star.
When the white dwarf reaches the Chandrasekhar mass limit, it can explode in a Type Ia supernova and produce the \pnucn{} by the \gprn{} \citep{travaglioTypeIaSupernovae2011, travaglioTestingRoleSNe2015}.
Rapidly accreting white dwarfs that have \ipr{} nucleosynthesis are also able to produce the \pnucn{} by the \gprn{} in the Type Ia supernovae \citep{battinoHeavyElementsNucleosynthesis2020}.
White dwarf explosions are able to produce the \pnucn{} apart from \indium[113], \tin[115], \lanthanum[138], \gadolinium[152], and \tantalum[180m] without an underproduction of the molybdenum and ruthenium isotopes \citep{rauscherConstrainingAstrophysicalOrigin2013}.

It has also been found that the \pnucn{} can be produced in the O-C shell merger of a massive star before the supernova explosion \citep{rauscherNucleosynthesisMassiveStars2002,ritterConvectivereactiveNucleosynthesisSc2018,robertiGprocessNucleosynthesisCorecollapse2023}.
As explained in Section \ref{sec:oc_mergers_intro}, the stable isotopes in the C-shell are ingested into the much hotter temperatures of the O-shell.
In the O-shell, the stable isotopes undergo photo-disintegration reactions and produce the \pnucn{}.
A significant percentage of the final yields of the \pnucn{} with $A>100$ are produced during the O-C shell merger independent of the peak explosion energy of the subsequent supernova \citep{robertiGprocessNucleosynthesisCorecollapse2023, robertiGprocessNucleosynthesisCorecollapse2024b}.
