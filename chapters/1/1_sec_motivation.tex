The origin of the elements in the universe is a fundamental question in astrophysics. 
The elements are produced in stars by various nuclear reactions during their lifetimes and deaths. 
Nuclear astrophysics studies what specific processes are responsible for the synthesis of the elements. 
The seminal work done by \cite{burbidgeSynthesisElementsStars1957} laid the foundation for nuclear astrophysics, describing the formation of all isotopes in stars via a series of burning stages and nuclear processes, and proposing likely stellar conditions where they happen.
Shortly after, \cite{henyeyNewMethodAutomatic1964} introduced a numerical method to model helium formation in stars via the CNO cycle. 
Since then, computational nuclear astrophysics has progressed significantly, enabling detailed modeling of stellar evolution across a wide range of masses and metallicities \citep{pignatariNuGridStellarData2016, ritterNuGridStellarData2018, battinoNuGridStellarData2019}.

\section{Motivation}

As far as computational nuclear astrophysics has come, there are still open questions regarding the origin of all isotopes.
The stellar models that calculate the nucleosynthesis are typically one dimensional, relying on the assumptions of spherical symmetry and mixing length theory to describe the convective motions in stars.
While these assumptions are adequate for much of the star's life, they do not hold as the star reaches the end of its life, particularly for massive stars.
The goal of this thesis is to investigate how the nucleosynthesis of isotopes during the late stages changes when taking into account the three dimensional nature of stars.

%One-dimensional (1D) stellar evolution models rely on assumptions of spherical symmetry and mixing length theory. While these assumptions are adequate for early nuclear burning stages, they break down during advanced burning phases \citep{bazanConvectionNucleosynthesisCore1994}. In particular, the convective O-burning shell and its potential merger with the convective C-burning shell, which occur shortly before core collapse, require three-dimensional (3D) hydrodynamic modeling to capture complex macrophysical flows \citep{meakinTurbulentConvectionStellar2007, jonesIdealizedHydrodynamicSimulations2017, andrassy3DHydrodynamicSimulations2020}. This complexity poses challenges for nucleosynthesis calculations, as asymmetric burning hotspots can lead to significant differences in elemental production compared to 1D models \citep{bazanConvectionNucleosynthesisCore1994, rizzutiShellMergersLate2024a}.

%The \pnucn{} are 35 neutron-rich stable isotopes heavier than iron, largely not produced by neutron capture processes \citep{burbidgeSynthesisElementsStars1957, woosleyPprocessesSupernovae1978}. Although several astrophysical sites have been proposed for their origin \citep{woosleyAlphaProcessRProcess1992, schatzRpprocessNucleosynthesisExtreme1998, rauscherNucleosynthesisMassiveStars2002, travaglioTestingRoleSNe2015}, no single site has been shown to produce all of the \pnucn{}. The O-burning shell of massive stars, first suggested in the context of core-collapse supernovae by \cite{woosleyPprocessesSupernovae1978}, is one of the few sites capable of producing nearly all \pnucn{}. Recent studies have shown that pre-explosive O-shell burning, particularly during O-C shell mergers, can synthesize these nuclei \citep{rauscherNucleosynthesisMassiveStars2002, ritterConvectivereactiveNucleosynthesisSc2018, robertiGprocessNucleosynthesisCorecollapse2023}. Furthermore, nucleosynthesis in the O-C merger dominates \pnucn{} production regardless of supernova explosion energy \citep{robertiGprocessNucleosynthesisCorecollapse2024b}, emphasizing the importance of this site. The O-C merger is also critical for producing odd-Z isotopes such as P, Cl, K, and Sc, which are underproduced in galactic chemical evolution models \citep{ritterConvectivereactiveNucleosynthesisSc2018, robertiOccurrenceImpactCarbonOxygen2025}.

%The O-C shell merger is inherently a 3D hydrodynamic environment, characterized by large asymmetric flows and localized burning, which are not captured by 1D models. This limitation hinders our understanding of nucleosynthesis in this region, as current 1D models rely on mixing length theory to describe convective motions. The purpose of this work is to investigate the impact of varying mixing conditions in the O-shell during C-shell entrainment on nucleosynthesis, with a particular focus on the \pnucn{}.
